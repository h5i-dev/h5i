The flight recorder: five lanes and three colours

Most dashboards use colour to tell you how worried to be. This one uses it to tell you how much the screen actually knows, which turns out to be the more useful question and a much harder one to design for.

By Koukyosyumei Reading time 7 min Tags Console · Evidence · Design
How to read it. h5i ui shows every box on the left and the selected box on the right, with a flight recorder of one row per receipt across five lanes: FS, NET, PROC, RES, PAGE. Red means the egress allowlist refused a destination, host-observed. Amber means something to look at that says nothing about containment. Grey means the evidence itself is weak. Clicking a row shows the rendered receipt, the same text h5i box inspect prints.

Here is the design problem. You are showing a reviewer twenty boxes and you have, for each one, a pile of execution records of varying reliability. Colour is the strongest signal a screen has, and there is exactly one convention for it: red is bad, green is fine, amber is in between.

That convention is wrong for this data, in a way that is worth being specific about. It maps colour to severity, and severity is a judgement. The moment a dashboard renders a judgement it has to defend the judgement, and the first surprising red becomes an argument about the thresholds rather than an investigation.

So the console maps colour to something else.

Red is one claim, and only one

Red appears when the egress allowlist refused a destination, observed host-side by the proxy. That is the only thing on the screen that makes a claim about the boundary, so it is the only thing that gets the strongest colour.

A failing test is not red. A crash is not red. A command with a suspicious-looking name is not red. Those are all things a reasonable person might want highlighted, and every one of them would dilute the single signal that means the containment boundary was touched.

The discipline here is unglamorous and it is the whole value. A colour that means five things means nothing.

Amber is "look at this", explicitly not "this is unsafe"

Amber covers a run that exited non-zero, a run the wall-clock limit killed, and browser errors reported from inside the box. Useful, frequently something to act on, and carrying no information about containment whatsoever.

Keeping that separate from red is what stops a busy screen from teaching people to ignore colour. A build that fails twice a day is amber twice a day, and it never trains anyone to scroll past the thing that means something else.

Grey is the interesting one

Grey means the evidence is weak, and it fires in two cases: the tier was workspace, so nothing was confined at all, or every receipt came from the in-box shim, so everything you are looking at is the box's own account of itself.

Grey is not "fine". It is "you cannot tell from here", and a dashboard that can say that is doing something most cannot. The failure mode it prevents is specific and common: a box that ran unconfined produces a clean-looking screen, because nothing was blocked, because nothing was blocking. Without a grey state that box would render identically to one that passed through a real boundary with nothing to report.

The same idea repeats at row level. Every run row is labelled host-observed or box-claimed, so the grade of the evidence travels with the event instead of being inferred from a legend somewhere else.

The five lanes

LaneWhat lands thereCan it be red?
FSFilesystem activity inside the policy's grants.No
NETEgress verdicts, including every refused destination.Yes, this is the only one
PROCCommands and exit codes, each with its observing lane.No, amber on non-zero
RESUsage against the profile's limits, including a wall-clock kill.No, amber on a kill
PAGEConsole errors, uncaught exceptions and failed requests from the in-box browser.No, amber on errors

Alongside the recorder, the right-hand pane shows the policy that was actually enforced, the services the box declares, and its diffstat against the pinned base. Actually enforced matters: on macOS at the kernel tiers a memory limit cannot be held, so it is marked rather than listed, and a screen showing the requested policy would be showing a wish.

Nothing on the screen is a score

There is no composite number, no letter grade, and no ranking. Counting is arithmetic over receipts and stays checkable; a score is a compression using weights nobody can audit, which turns a surprising result into something to explain away.

h5i shipped a scoring module once. It was deleted, and the console was rebuilt on counting. If you want a ranking, you write it yourself out of counts you can see, which means you can also state it and change it.

It cannot press anything

Every route is a GET. shell, run, export, propose, apply and rm stay in the CLI where a human types them. The server binds 127.0.0.1 and nothing else, and the URL carries a token minted for the session and kept in memory, never written to disk, so no box can read it. The page trades it for a strict same-site cookie on first load and cross-origin requests are refused.

A console with no mutating surface has no mutating surface to guard, which is a smaller and more defensible thing to have built.

What it will not tell you

  • Whether a change is safe. The screen shows boundary events. Whether what you find is malicious, careless or entirely normal is your call.
  • That a quiet box is a good box. The most dangerous diff in a release can come out of a box that did nothing unusual.
  • Anything at all about egress at the microvm tier. The guest's netstack drops packets without reporting them, so NET has nothing to draw.

FAQ

What do red, amber and grey mean in the h5i console?

Red means the egress allowlist refused a destination, observed host-side by the proxy, and it is the only signal that makes a claim about the boundary. Amber means something to look at, such as a non-zero exit, a wall-clock kill or browser errors, with no containment meaning. Grey means the evidence is weak.

Why is grey the most useful colour?

Because it says you cannot tell from here, which most dashboards cannot express. It fires when the tier was workspace so nothing was confined, or when every receipt came from the in-box shim so everything shown is the box's own account. Without it, an unconfined box would render identically to a confined one with nothing to report.

What are the five lanes?

FS for filesystem activity, NET for egress verdicts, PROC for commands and exit codes, RES for usage against the profile's limits, and PAGE for what the in-box browser reported. Only NET can go red.

Can the console change anything about a box?

No. Every route is a GET, and shell, run, export, propose, apply and remove all stay in the CLI. The server binds loopback only and the URL carries a session token kept in memory rather than on disk, so no box can read it.
